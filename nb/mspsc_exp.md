# Designing a synaptic event experiment
There are a variety of ways you can record synaptic events.  We can record synpatic currents or potentials.  Synaptic currents are recorded in voltage-clamp so we can clamp cells at a specific voltage and also record currents from specific receptor types. These are probably the most common type of synaptic event recording. Synaptic potentials are recorded in current-clamp and you generally let the cell float at the resting membrane potential.

## PSCs
Postsynaptic currents are recorded in voltage-clamp. Voltage clamp means that the amplifier will inject current into the cell to keep it at your choosen holding voltage. Any changes in current means that the cells had a change in voltage due to a current that the amplifier is counteracting. When you get an inward current of positive ions the amplifier will inject a negative current to keep the cell at your chosen holding voltage. PSCs can come from a variety of receptors; AMPA, NMDA, GABAA, glycine, etc. If you see EPSC then that usually refers to AMPA mPSCs and if you see IPSC that generally refers to GABAA PSCs. However, it is always good to specify what receptor you are recording from and what voltage you are clamping the neuron at. The internal solution you use will determine what voltage you can record specific currents at. For example, a standard cesium internal solution will allow you to record AMPA PSCs at -70 mV. A low-chloride cesium internal will allow you to record AMPA PSCs at -70 mV and GABAA at -70 mV.

You can record quantal, non-quantal, evoked and uncaged synaptic events. For PSCs you can use either cesium or potassium internals. However, if you want very clean PSCs I suggest using a cesium internal with QX-314 (Br). Cesium blocks potassium channels and QX-314 blocks voltage-gated sodium channels which will greatly improve the space clamp on the neuron. The better space clamp you have the more you can detect synaptic events on the distal parts of the dendrites.

### Quantal events
Quantal events are non-evoked, synaptic events elicited by a single synaptic vesicle [@delcastilloQuantalComponentsEndplate1954]. You will need to recorded in voltage clamp and have TTX in the bath. Quantal events are generally termed mPSCs. mPSCs are used to quantify the relative amount of synaptic input onto a cell. THe higher mPSC frequency the higher the synaptic input. Recording from different receptors tells you different things about your cells. AMPAR mPSCs are used to for total excitatory input onto a cell. NMDAR mPSCs are used as a proxy for silent synapses or synaptic maturation. GABAAR mPSCs are used for total GABAergic inhibitory input onto a cell.

### Spontaneous events
Spontaneous events are non-evoked, single synaptic events recorded without TTX. Spontaneous events are generally termed sPSC. You will need to record in voltage clamp. sPSCs are non-quantal events meaning that synaptic currents can come from the release of more than one synaptic at a single dendritic spine. sPSCs are very useful in when studying the effects of drugs that alter cell function. Changes in sPSC frequency due to drug wash in suggest that the presynaptic neuron is affected by whatever drug you applied to the bath. If you just see a change in the holding current (the amount of current it takes to hold your cell at the mV you are holding it at) or the amplitude of synaptic events then there is likely a postsynaptic effect of the drug. sPSCs can also be used as a first pass for synaptic input onto a cell but have a lot more caveats than mPSCs.

### Evoked events
Evoked events events are synpatic events where you use ChR2 or electrical stimulation to evoked release of many synapses.

#### Electrical stimulation
Electrical stimulation will stimulate all the synapses in a small region around the electrode to release synaptics. The more current you use the more synapses might release synaptic vesicles. Electrical stimulation can be very variable.


There are two primary ways you can record mPSCs. One way is you can record continuously for about 3-5 minutes. Technically speaking this is the easiest method since you have a single recording and pretty much any simple recording software will implement this method. The second way is you can record 20-40 sweeps/acquisitions of 5-15 seconds each. Each of these acquisitions act as a kind of technical replicate. This method allows you to discard bad portions of the recording. Usually when you get proficient at patching you will rarely have bad recordings however sometimes you get 30 seconds where there is an unstable seal, digital cell phone noise, your bath gets too low, you get pump/vacuum noise or other issues. When this happens it is fine to discard the 5 or so acquisitions that are bad. You can create acquisitions from continuous recordings by splitting to get the same benefits of the sweeps/acquisitions method.

Filtering and sample rate are the other important considerations for capturing mPSCs. You generally want the sample rate to be 3-4x greater than the filter cutoff you are using with the filter cutoff determining the high frequency you are interested in. While signal theory says you have to have the sample rate 2x greater than the high frequency you are interested in, generally to capture that high frequency well you need to sample 3-4x times that rate to get a good resolution signal. In general mPSCs are recorded at a 10000 Hz with a 3000 Hz lowpass cutoff. A 10000 Hz sample rate is high enough to capture the rise of a mPSCs which are fairly quick, especially for mEPSCs on parvalbumin interneurons. 10000 Hz is also a good trade off between accuracy of the signal and digital storage space. In modern times you could realistically record at 20000 Hz with no storage space issues. I suggest a mininum sample rate of 10000 Hz.
