# Preparing for and designing an electrophysiology experiment
Before you start any experiment you should really lay out the goals you want to achieve and see whether your experiment will achieve those goals. Designing an experiment to see if there are genotypes differences may be different when designing an experiment to test for effects of a GPCR agonist. There are also certain quality checks you need to make for every experiment.

## What types of experiments can we do?
Some of these experiments will be covered in the data analysis sections but this is a quick overview of what you can do. Several experiments can be done simultaneously. Some expriments require specific drugs, internal or types of ACSF.
- **Structural synaptic connectivity**: mPSCs, optogenetic stimulation or electric stimulation. All of these will test "structural" synaptic connectivity. mPSCs you are looking at quantal events (using TTX in the bath) and general synaptic input. Optogenetic stimulation is good for projection specific input but comes with expression issues and stimulation issues. You can get "quantal" events from optogenetic stimulation using a strontium-based ACSF but the data I have seen is always noisy. For these experiments you want to use a Cs+-based internal if possible to provide better space clamp.
- **sEPSC**: Non-quantal synaptic events, used to study presynaptic and postsynaptic senstivity to drugs or for preliminary synaptic connectivity when doing current clamp recordings in the same cell. Usually you wash a drug on and see how frequency (presynaptic) and amplitude (postsynaptic) change over time.
- **Synaptic plasticity**: Long-term potentiation (LTP) and Long-term depression (LTD). How synaptic strength (amplitude of evoked events) changes due to high or low frequency input. There are different protocols depedending on the cell type you are recording.
- **Excitability**: Step or ramp current injection used to test the rheobase, or how easily a cell spikes. Lower rheobase means a cell is more likely to fire with less synaptic input. FI curve analysis to breakdown any changes in gain. Changes in the FI curve related to spiking dynamic range of a neuron.
- **Membrane properties**: Small current or voltage injections although small current injections are more common. Used to measure membrane resistance, membrane tau and capacitance. Very useful for building neural models.
- **Channel specific currents (like Ih)**: Typically done in voltage clamp. Voltage steps can get pretty crazy if you need to test the voltage responsiveness of certain channels.
- **Synaptic multiplicity**: Ratio of mEPSC to sEPSC. This is a pretty esoteric experiment but there are some who claim that this shows the relative amount of synapses that connect with two dendritic spines.
- **Synaptic release probability**: paired pulse ratio using optogenetic or electrical stimulation. You can also do less common experiments by changing the Ca++ in the external bath or even stimulate Ca++ release from the internal stores.
- **Synaptic rundown**: Looks at repeated synaptic stimulation and recovery using either optogenetic or more commonly electrical stimulation.
- **Membrane resonance**: Chirp current injections to test what frequencies the membrane resonates at (impeadance). Synaptic input at a resonant frequency can induce larger changing in membrane voltage.
-**Nonlinear resonance**: Pink or white noise current injections to simulate synaptic "noise". Pink is noise is the average noise in the brain and white noise is usually used for testing time based noise effects that are related to membrane resonance. These get at how the cell is likely to integrate synaptic input in an intact brain. Useful for building neural models.
- **Glutamate uncaging**: Very useful for testing dendritic integration, synaptic function or even testing how expansive the dendritic tree is. You need a 2P microscope to run these experiments so they are less common.

## What parameters are common to all experiments?
There are several parameters you will want to keep track of for every cell and all experiments. I recommend setting up a spreadsheet to collect all these parameters. It keeps the data structured and legible and you can do it on a computer or print out a sheet for your notebook.
- **Access/series resistance**: Often termed Ra or Rs (hereafter termed Ra) in the software. This is the resistance of the pipette-cell membrane interface. Ra is measured using a test pulse which is a small voltage step in voltage clamp. You want this lower than 20 MOhm and ideally <20% of you cell's membrane resistance. You also want Ra to change no more than about 20% over your experiment. Ra filters or truncates current which is why we want is as low as possible. Some labs use series resistance subtraction to remove the effect of Ra. With this you still need to ensure Ra does not change and it can induce oscillations in the signal. I would not personally use this but some scientists are adament that you use it. Ra is typically calculated in voltage clamp with a small voltage injection. I track Ra throughout an experiment and before and after every recording I do on a cell. You can also setup a test pulse to run after every acquisition if you are doing episodic recordings (also called sweeps). Ra is determined by the size if the shaft of the pipette (heavily determined by the angle), the size of the tip and the internal solution you are using. Internal solutions with a high concentration of small ionic species, like a 2M KCl sharp electrode solution, will have a lower Ra compared to a KMeSO3 solution when using the same pipette. Pipettes with a longer, narrower shaft will have a higher Ra. Pipettes with a smaller diameter opening will have a higher Ra. 
- **Membrane resistance**: You can get a rough measure in voltage clamp using a small voltage step. This is just the resistance of the membrane and is related to excitability. If you are using as cesium-based internal you need to get this value as soon as you break in since cesium will depolarize your cell as it blocks potassium channels.
- **Capacitance**: You can get a rough measure in voltage clamp using a small voltage step. Capacitance is related to the size of the cell and is important for membrane resonance since it contributes to impeadance. Capacitance measured using a test pulse in voltage clamp and I have found is highly dependent on the access/series resistance. If you are using a cesium-based internal you need to get this value as soon as you break in. Capacitance also changes over time if your cell swells or shrinks so it is a good idea to get this measure as soon as you break-in.
- **Membrane voltage**: This is less common but not hard to get. You can just switch to I=0 mode and record the voltage. This is a rough/noisy estimate but usually good enough. If you are using as cesium-based internal you need to get this value as soon as you break in. Membrane voltage is good to monitor through out your experiment if you are using a potassium-based internal. If a cell depolarizes then it has likely died and a good reason to remove it from your analysis.
- **Bath temperature**: You should record and note the bath temperature your experiments are done at. I recommend just recording everything at $\text{32\textdegree}$ which is considered physiological temperature but you still need to monitor the bath temperature through all your recordings.

## Less common parameters but still important
- **Low magnification image of cell location**: Image of where in the slice you recorded your cell. Do this after recording your cell and before you move or withdraw your pipette from the tissue. Very uncommon but you can actually use this to map depth from cortical surface, location around NAc or VTA, etc. Also, this is visual proof that you are recording cells where you state they are. I think this is something everyone should just collect but if definitely is not common to do so.
- **Pipette offset**: This is the voltage offset needed to reduce the current between your pipette and ACSF when in the bath. I think it is good to keep track of this because it should be consistent when using the same internal and external solution. You will likely never be asked for this by reviewers but it is a good quality control measure.
- **Voltage clamp holding voltage**: Usually this is protocol based but if you ever switch between different voltages to test AMPA/NMDA currents or mEPSC/mIPSCs within the same cell then it can be beneficial to keep track of what holding voltage you are using. Usually the currents go in a different direction which is also an indicator of what you may record but it cannot hurt to explicity state this in your notebook.
- **Voltage clamp holding current**: This is the amount of current needed to hold your cell at a particular voltage when in voltage clamp. This is more of a quality control metric. It will depend on the cell type you are recording and the internal you are using. You want this consistent within a cell type using the same internal. The holding current can also change when you perfuse drugs or even just change over time which is important. Lastly if you inject a slow (DC) current when in current clamp to hold your cell at the same voltage, the holding current will be that current.
- **Current clamp holding current**: This is the slow (DC) current you inject into a cell to try to keep it at a specific voltage when in current clamp. Some labs inject no current so you may not need it. It is just another quality control.
- **Access resistance subtraction**: If you are compensating for access/series resistance write down how much you are compensating which is usually a percentage and the bandwidth (at least on Multiclamp Commander).
- **Whole cell compensation**: If you compensate for whole cell (Ra and Cm) then write down the capacitance and resistance settings. I have publications where the authors use it but I have never seen the values that are reported. It is probably a good quality control metric. One note on whole cell compensation is that it assumes that your Ra and Cm are not chaning over time which is a big assumption to make. It can also induce oscillations in your recordings.
- **Pipette capcitance neutralization**: Some amplifier software includes this in current clamp mode. I would keep track of how much you are using.
- **Pipette resistance**: Good to keep track to make sure you are consistent throughout your experiments. Also good to see of your pipette puller is not working or if the filament needs to be changed.
- **Filter**: Filter settings are usually set and forget but it cannot hurt to write down somewhere what you use. Usually a 3k Bessel or Butterworth filter are used in voltage clamp. Filtering generally depends on signal to noise ratio (SNR). mEPSCs have low SNR where as standard current injections to study rheobase and spiking behavior have high SNR which is why you typically see filtering for the former but not the latter.
- **Gain**: You usually don't have to adjust gain unless you are getting LFPs or have small currents but write it down if you do since some DIY recording programs don't get the gain values from amplifier software. Gain will change the units (*i.e.* milli vs micro) of your signal.
